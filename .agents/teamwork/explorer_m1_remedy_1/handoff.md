## 2. Logic Chain

1. From Observation 1.1, `f.finishedAt`, `oflat.startedAt`, and `oflat.estimatedFinishAt` store timestamps produced by `new Date().toISOString()`. This produces UTC timestamps ending in `Z`.
2. Brazil local time (`America/Sao_Paulo`) is UTC-3 (standard time, without DST).
3. Any event occurring between 21:00:00 and 23:59:59 local Brazil time occurs between 00:00:00 and 02:59:59 UTC of the *following* calendar day.
4. When comparing or extracting dates via naive `.substring(0, 10)`:
   - At line 8159, a flat finished at 21:30 BRT on September 30 has UTC timestamp `2026-10-01T00:30:00.000Z`. Its `.substring(0, 10)` is `"2026-10-01"`. Meanwhile, `todayStr` is `"2026-09-30"`. The equality check evaluates to `false`.
   - Consequently, the worker's completed flat is omitted from `doneTodayCount`, allowing the worker to exceed `maxFlatsPerDay` indefinitely between 21:00 and 23:59:59.
   - On the next day (October 1), `todayStr` becomes `"2026-10-01"`. The equality check `f.finishedAt.substring(0, 10) === todayStr` now evaluates to `true`, incorrectly consuming 1 flat quota from October 1's allowance before any work has occurred.
   - At lines 9981–9982, for a flat in progress at 21:30 BRT on September 30, `startDate` and `endDate` are evaluated as `"2026-10-01"`. When the PMS calendar queries the range `["2026-09-30", "2026-09-30"]`, `startDate <= end` (`"2026-10-01" <= "2026-09-30"`) is `false`. The service block fails to display on the calendar on the day the work is actually being performed.
5. In Observation 1.1 item 3, `getExecutionDateStr(isoString)` uses `BRAZIL_DATE_FORMATTER`, which evaluates `new Date(isoString)` in `America/Sao_Paulo`. For `2026-10-01T00:30:00.000Z`, it returns `"2026-09-30"`.
6. Therefore, substituting `getExecutionDateStr(f.finishedAt)` at line 8159 and `getExecutionDateStr(oflat.startedAt)` / `getExecutionDateStr(oflat.estimatedFinishAt)` at lines 9981–9982 completely eliminates both bugs.
7. From Observation 1.1 item 4, if `getExecutionDateStr` receives a string that is already date-only (`YYYY-MM-DD`), Node.js parses it as UTC midnight and rolls it back 3 hours into the previous day. Adding a fast regex guard `/^\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}$/` ensures idempotency: date-only strings are preserved as-is, while ISO timestamps with time are accurately converted to Brazil local date.
